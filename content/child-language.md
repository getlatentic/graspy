# How Aunty Chioma talks to children

Every line a lesson speaks follows this guide. `dashboard/tests/test_child_language.py` checks the
rules that can be measured; a plan that breaks one does not build. The rest is for the author and the
reviewer.

## Who is listening

| Band | Classes | Age | Reads? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early | Nursery 1, Nursery 2, Kindergarten | 3 to 5 | No |
| Lower primary | Primary 1 to 3 | 6 to 8 | A little, slowly |
| Upper primary | Primary 4 to 6 | 9 to 11 | Yes, but listens first |

Most children hear the lesson; few read it. Write for the ear.

## Rules the build checks

1. **Short, whole sentences.** At most 8 words a sentence for early learners, 10 for lower primary,
   14 for upper primary. A sentence is a real sentence: never cut one in two to pass this rule.
2. **Short lines.** A whole line holds at most 20 words for early learners, 30 for lower primary and
   40 for upper primary (60 when upper primary is taught something new). Many short sentences are
   better than a few long ones.
3. **No grown-up or computer words.** Never: recite, voice note, objective, quick check, by the end,
   fact, facts, correct, incorrect, attempt, recording, system, verdict.
4. **Numbers as words.** "Three times four is twelve", never "3 x 4 = 12". The screen shows digits.
5. **A turn ends with the one thing to do.** A line that asks the child to speak ends with a question
   or with what to say: "What is three times seven?" or "Say it with me: three times three is nine."
6. **Every sentence starts with a capital letter**, in every language: the words are shown on screen too.
7. **Pidgin and Yoruba stay close in length.** A translation may be at most half as long again as
   the English, so the child waits no longer in one language than another.

## How each part of a lesson sounds

Lessons follow Gagné's nine events. Children do not hear the names; they hear this:

| Event | What it does for the child | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| gain_attention | Something they know from home or school | "Three stones hold up the cooking pot. Let us count in threes!" |
| inform_objectives | What they will be able to do, said as good news | "Today you will learn the three times table. All by yourself!" |
| stimulate_recall | What they already know, as a friendly question | "Do you remember the two times table? What is two times three?" |
| present_content | One idea, shown with something they can picture | "Three groups of three stones. Three, six, nine. Three times three is nine." |
| provide_guidance | Doing it together, never a test | "Say it with me: three times three is nine." |
| elicit_performance | Their turn, alone, with where to start | "Now you say the three times table. Start with three times one." |
| provide_feedback | Mistakes are for fixing together | "Let us fix the ones you missed. Listen, then say them with me." |
| assess_performance | A friendly check, not an exam | "Let us see what you remember. What is three times seven?" |
| enhance_retention | Praise, then what comes next | "Well done! Tomorrow we say it again, then we start the four times table." |

## Times means groups of the first number

"Four times three" is three groups of four: four, eight, twelve. The four times table counts in fours,
so every times table is taught as groups of its own number, and the screen shows 4 × 3 = 12. Never
explain the same fact the other way round in the same lesson.

## Voice

- Warm and plain, like a kind aunty, never babyish. Praise the work: "You said every one", not "You are so clever".
- One idea at a time. If a line needs "and then" twice, it is two lines.
- Name things a Nigerian child sees: yam, pot, goat, mango, danfo, naira, market, fingers, stones.
- Say "we" for learning together and "you" for the child's turn.
- Wrong is never a failure: "Let us try that again" or "Listen: three times seven is twenty-one."

## Languages

- **English**: Nigerian classroom English, simple words.
- **Pidgin**: natural Nigerian Pidgin as a child hears it at home ("Na your turn now"), not English
  with a few Pidgin words. Maths words stay English: "three times four na twelve".
- **Yoruba**: full tone marks. Number words stay in English for mathematics, as children say them in
  school: "Kí ni three times four?"

A native speaker reviews every Pidgin and Yoruba line before it ships. Until then a line carries the
English meaning faithfully but is marked as a draft in the release notes.
